1. Building slightly more apartments (10%)…

  • …may not alleviate congestion!
    • Some agents wait longer
    • Vacancies rise; aftermarket prices hardly change
  • Inefficiency: Agents “hold out” for competitive developments
    • Lose future lotteries? Cannot take today’s apartments
    • Building more apartments can worsen this problem

2. Changing allocation rules for public housing…

  • … complements increasing supply

A strategyproof mechanism eliminating intertemporal risk…

  • makes poor/rich households wait 8%/23% less
  • reduces vacancies (12% \( \to \) 7%)
  • raises prices on the aftermarket (>12%)
    • advantages owner-occupiers over applicants
  • If we now build more apartments…
    • Wait times remain low
    • Relieves upward pressure on aftermarket prices

5. Model: Outline of Public Housing Market

  • New apartment supply exogenous;
  • Owner-occupiers sell or not;
  • Applicants enter one lottery, apply to clearinghouse, wait, or exit

5.1. Dynamic equilibrium model of housing choice

Every period, new housing developments arrive exogenously.

Existing owner-occupiers

  • Infinite horizon, perfect foresight through 2023
  • Given the prices they face, either
    1. Sell off their apartment (terminal); or
    2. Continue living in it
  • Key trade-off: Sell today vs. sell at higher price tomorrow

5.2. Applicants

  • Infinite horizon, perfect foresight
    • Differ by income, applicant age, family structure, and “hidden” region preferences
  • Given prefs, prices and odds of success over developments, either
    1. Apply for one project within the mechanism;
    2. Apply to one region in the clearinghouse;
    3. Buy a “used” apartment in some region; or
    4. Wait (live with family).
  • Key trade-off: Success odds vs. desirability

Markets clear via lottery odds (1\(^{\circ} \)) and prices (2\(^{\circ} \))

6. Model: Details

(FAST FORWARD: ESTIMATION) (FAST FORWARD: COUNTERFACTUALS)

6.1. Used apartments: Notation

Every quarter \( t \): owner-occupier \( i \) decides whether to sell off her apartment

  • Action 1/0: To sell/not
  • \( a \): type (location) of the used apartment
  • \( P_t(S; a) \): market price of apartments of type \( a \)
  • \( V_{it}[L, P(S; a)] \): value of owning type \( a \) apartment
    • Abusing algebra, suppress dependence on price path \( P(S; a) \)

6.2. Used apartments: Indirect utilities

Every quarter \( t \): owner-occupier \( i \) decides whether to sell off her apartment

  • Indirect utility from selling is \[ w_{it}(1; a) = \rho P_t(S; a) + \xi_{t}(1, S; a) + \epsilon_{it}(1, S; a) \]
  • Indirect utility from staying is \[ w_{it}(0; a) = \delta(S) \mathbb{E}[V_{it+1}(a)] + \xi_{t}(0, S; a) + \epsilon_{it}(0, S; a) \]

6.3. Used apartment: Choice probabilities and Supply

  • Conditional choice probability of type \( a \) to sell at time \( t \): \[ p_{t}(1; a, P(S)) = \frac{\exp[w_t(1; a, P(S))]}{\exp[w_t(0; a, P(S))] + \exp[w_t(1; a, P(S))]} \]
  • In region \( l \), supply of secondary market housing at time \( t\) is \[ \mathbb{S}_t( l, P(S)) = \int_{a \in l} p_{t}(1; a, P(S)) \, d\lambda_t(a), \] where \( \lambda_t(a) \) is the housing stock of type \( a \) apartments at time \( t \).

6.4. Model — Demand (Preferences)

Specify model backwards.

  • Applicant \( i \): 2-tuple \(\omega_i = (\zeta_i, s_i) \), (obs. char., unobs. region. pref.)
  • BTO project \( j \): 4-tuple \((l_{jt}, x_{jt}, P_{jt}, \xi_{jt}) \)
    • (region, obs. char., price vector by type, unobs. amenities by type)
    • \( L_{jt} \): 5-vector of regions, with entries \( \mathbb{1}\{ l_{jt} = l \}\)
    • Probability of success at \( j \): \( r_{jt}(\omega) \)
  • Clearinghouse option \((C, l)\): region \( l \), price vector \( P_t(C, l; \omega) \)
    • Probability of success: \( r_{t}(C, l; \omega) \)
  • Exit option \((E, l)\): region \( l \), price vector \( P_t(E, l; \omega) \)

6.5. Flow payoffs (1)

If applicant \( i \) of type \( \omega \) remains unmatched, her flow utility is \[ u_t(0; \omega) = \xi_t(W; \omega) + \delta \mathbb{E}V_{t+1}(\omega). \]

Else her terminal utilities from success are:

  • Apartment in BTO project \( j \): \[ u_t(j; \omega) = L_{jt}'\color{blue}{\phi(\omega)} + x_{jt}'\color{blue}{\beta(\omega)} - \color{blue}{\alpha(\omega)} P_{jt}(\omega) + \color{blue}{\gamma(\omega)} \mathbb{E}V_{t'}[j; R, \omega] + \xi_{t}(j; \omega) \]
    • \( \phi(\omega) \): Governs preference for project \( j \) being in \( i \)’s preferred region(s)
    • \( \beta(\omega) \): Governs preferences over other observables of project \( j \)
    • \( \alpha(\omega) \): Governs preferences over apartment price
    • \( \gamma(\omega) \): Governs preferences over “future value” of ownership
      • Kalouptsidi (2014): Proxy by forward resale price

6.6. Flow payoffs (2)

  • Apartment in BTO project \( j \): \[ u_t(j; \omega) = L_{jt}'\phi(\omega) + x_{jt}'\beta(\omega) - \alpha(\omega) P_{jt}(\omega) + \gamma(\omega) \mathbb{E}V_{t'}[j; R, \omega] + \xi_{t}(j; \omega) \]
  • Apartment on clearinghouse in region \( l \): \[ u_t(C, l; \omega) = L(C, l)'\phi(\omega) - \alpha(\omega) P_{t}(C, l; \omega) + \xi_{t}(C, l; \omega) \]
  • Apartment on secondary market in region \( l \): \[ u_t(E, l; \omega) = L(E, l)'\phi(\omega) - \alpha(\omega) P_t(E, l; \omega) \]

6.7. Choice-specific value functions

  • BTO apartment \( j \): \[ v_{it}(j; \omega) = r_{t}(j; \omega) u_t(j; \omega) + [1-r_{t}(j; \omega)] u_t(0; \omega) + \epsilon_{it}(j; \omega) \]
  • Clearinghouse, region \( l \): \[ v_{it}(C, l; \omega) = r_{t}(C, l; \omega) u_t(C, l; \omega) + [1-r_{t}(C, l; \omega)] u_t(0; \omega) + \epsilon_{it}(C, l; \omega) \]
  • Apartment on secondary market, region \( l \): \[ v_{it}(E, l; \omega) = u_t(E, l; \omega) + \epsilon_{it}(E, l; \omega) \]
  • Wait one period: \[ v_{it}(W; \omega) = u_t(0; \omega) + \epsilon_{it}(W; \omega). \]

6.8. Household decision problem

  • If unmatched, decides optimally: \[ V_t(\omega) = \mathbb{E}\left\{\max_{d \in \mathcal{J}_t \cup \mathcal{C}_t \cup \mathcal{E}_t \cup \{ W\}} \mathbb{E}_t[v_{it}(d; \omega)] \right\} \]
  • Possible actions:
    • \( d \in \mathcal{J}_t \): apply to BTO project \( d \)
    • \( d \in \mathcal{C}_t \): apply to clearinghouse, region \( d \)
    • \( d \in \mathcal{E}_t \): take used apartment, region \( d \)
    • \( d = W \): wait one period

6.9. Market shares

  • Type-specific market share of BTO project \( j \) at time \( t \): \[ p_{t}(j; \omega) = \frac{\exp(\bar v_{t}(j; \omega))}{\sum_{l \in \mathcal{L}}[\exp(\bar v_{t}(C, l; \omega)) + \exp(\bar v_{t}(E, l; \omega))] + \exp(\bar v_{t}(W; \omega)) + \sum_{k \in \mathcal{J}_t} \exp(\bar v_{t}(k; \omega))} \]

6.10. Model — Equilibrium

In equilibrium,

  1. [Young households apply/wait/exit optimally]
  2. [Owner-occupiers sell/remain optimally]
  3. [Success probabilities in the mechanism are consistent]
  4. [Sequence of secondary market prices sets demand equal to supply]

8. Estimation: Details

8.1. Supply estimation: Outline

Want to recover how sensitive each household’s moving decision is to changes in their apartment’s price.

  • IV-FE regression over each (neighborhood, apartment size) pair (Euler CCP; Kalouptsidi, Scott and Souza-Rodrigues 2021)
    • Avoids repeatedly iterating value functions
  • Endogeneity: Price correlated with unobservables
  • Instruments: Demand shocks
    • Expansion of eligibility for BTO (2015, 2019)
    • (Q: Other supply shifters?)
  • Identification: \( \Delta \) market shares and prices by apartment type over time
  • Results: Elasticity of 3.64 (se: 1.59)
    • Higher than new construction (1.75; Saiz 2010) and housing unit provision (0.3; Baum-Snow and Lu 2021)

Supply estimation: Hotz-Miller inversion

By a Hotz-Miller (1993) inversion, the relative attractiveness of selling vs. staying is \[ \Delta_{1,0} \ln s_{t|l} = \Delta_{1,0} \bar w_{t|l} = \rho_0 \bar p^S_{t|l} + x_{1t|l}'\rho_1 + \Delta_{1,0} \xi_{t|l} - \delta^o \mathbb{E}V^o_{t+1|l}.\]

  • Selling is a terminal action (Arcidiacono and Miller 2011)
    • \( \mathbb{E}V^o_{t+1|l} = \gamma^E + \rho_0 \bar p^o_{t+1|l} + x_{1t+1|l}'\rho_1 + \xi_{1t+1|l} - \ln s_{1t+1|l} \)

Identification — Dynamic Supply

\begin{align*} (\Delta_{1,0} \ln s_{t|l}) - \delta^o \ln s_{1t+1|l} &= - \delta^o \gamma^E + \rho_0(\bar p^S_{t|l} - \delta^o \bar p^S_{t+1|l}) \\ &+ (x_{1t|l} - \delta^o x_{1t+1|l})'\rho_1 + \underbrace{(-\delta^o\xi_{1t+1|l} + \Delta_{1,0} \xi_{t|l})}_{\tilde \xi_{t|l}} \end{align*}
  • Mean resale prices \( \bar p^S_{t|l} \) possibly correlated with composite unobservable \( \tilde \xi_{t|l} \).
    • E.g. Work from home trend: agents in larger apartments less likely to vacate, driving prices up but lowering fraction of sellers
  • Instrument with (demand-side) policy shocks in 08/2015, 07/2018 and 08/2019.
    • The government unexpectedly
      • (2015, 2019) raised subsidies + expanded eligibility for BTO
      • (2018) tightened Loan-to-Value limits
    • Plausibly uncorrelated with period unobservable factors that influence the probability of sale

8.2. Demand estimation: Outline

Demand estimation combines techniques from labor and IO.

  1. Perform data-driven estimation of latent demand types (k-means; e.g., Bonhomme, Lamadon, and Manresa 2019; 2022). Obtain five applicant “types”
  2. Form conditional choice probabilities by applicant type
  3. Recover determinants of applicant utility
    1. Use finite dependence (e.g., Arcidiacono and Miller 2011) to “cancel” forward-looking part of utility
    2. Recover weights on prices + amenities using IV (Hotz and Miller 1993; Berry 1994; Kalouptsidi, Scott, and Souza-Rodrigues 2021)

8.3. Demand estimation: (1) Data-driven estimation of latent demand types

Challenge
Rich data on applicant demographics and application history. “Let data speak” to which applicants are similar.
Solution
Cluster via k-means (Lloyd 1982).
  • Characteristics: Household income, age of main applicant, maximal number of children in sample, modal region of application, length of time from first to last application, etc.

8.4. Demand estimation: (2) Estimating preferences type-by-type

Once types/CCPs are recovered, applicant preferences are obtained by standard IV techniques. Fix a type \( \omega \) and a time period \( t \); suppress subscripts.

  • Take two BTO projects \( j, k \in \mathcal{J}_{t} \).
  • Construct the object \[ \frac{1}{1 - q(j)} v(j) = \frac{q(j)}{1-q(j)} u(j) + u(0) \]
    • Continuation value only appears in \( u(0) \)
  • Take differences between \( j \) and \( k \): \[ \frac{1}{1 - q(j)} \ln s(j) - \frac{1}{1 - q(k)} \ln s(k) = \frac{q(j)}{1-q(j)} u(j) - \frac{q(k)}{1-q(k)} u(k) \]

8.5. Demand estimation: Recover preferences

\[ \frac{1}{1 - q(j)} \ln s(j) - \frac{1}{1 - q(k)} \ln s(k) = \frac{q(j)}{1-q(j)} u(j) - \frac{q(k)}{1-q(k)} u(k) \]

  • Does not depend on continuation value!
  • Estimate by IV regression (exploit exogenous shocks to BTO prices in 2015/2019)
    • Variation: Between developments within period
    • Obtain: \( \hat \phi(\omega), \hat \beta(\omega), \hat \alpha(\omega), \hat \gamma(\omega) \)

8.6. Demand estimation: Recover structural residuals

  • Exiting is terminal. For any region \( l \), tomorrow’s value: \[ \hat V' = \gamma(E) + L(E, l)^T \hat \phi - \hat\alpha p'(E, l) - \ln s'(E, l) \]
  • Compare waiting and exiting today: \[ \ln s(W) - \ln s(E, l) = \xi(W) + \delta \hat V' - L(E, l)^T \hat \phi + \hat \alpha p(E, l) \]
  • Solve for \( \hat \xi(W) \).
  • Finally, compare applying to \( j \) and exiting: \[ \ln s(j) - \ln s(E, l) = \text{known stuff} + q(j) \xi(j) + (1-q(j)) \left[ \hat \xi(W) + \delta \hat{V}' \right] \]
  • Solve for \( \hat \xi(j) \).

Caution: Subsequent results are/use old estimates.

9.1. Overview of counterfactuals

Today: With old estimates, compare supply increase with new rules.

  • Supply: 10% supply increase in oversubscribed projects
  • New rules: Applicants submit preferences over (future) projects and exiting; lottery over applicants clears the market

In progress: Batching; planner’s problem; “better” pricing; welfare

  • New rules: Run BTO every 6 months (Ferdowsian, Lee, and Yap 2023)
  • Planner’s problem: Maximize utilitarian welfare s.t. applicants only matched to developments after they “become active”
  • Pricing: BTO + \( \Delta \) prices s.t. in each development, applicant:capacity is 1:1

9.2. Changing allocation rules

Eliminate intertemporal risk through a variant of RSD!

  1. Given guess of aftermarket prices, all agents truthfully list their preferences over (eligible) developments and exiting
  2. At random, each agent assigned preferred development with capacity

In simulations, find aftermarket prices to clear the market.

10. Conclusion

Main takeaways

  • Increasing supply is not a panacea for congestion
    • Demand response can eclipse the supply increase
  • Better rules can improve allocation outcomes…
    • but may redistribute
  • Changing rules complements increasing supply!

11. Appendix

11.1. Computing Differentiation IVs

  1. Predict endogenous prices
    • Flexible specification of instruments and exogenous variables
  2. For each characteristic \( k \) that is either exogenous or a predicted price,
    • Compute the isolation of development \( a \) in \( k \): \[ z_{at, kk} \triangleq \sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}_t; a' \neq a} (x_{at, k} - x_{a't, k})^2; \]
    • With another such characteristic \( k' \), their interaction: \[ z_{at,kk'} \triangleq \sum_{a' \in \mathcal{A}_t; a' \neq a} (x_{at, k} - x_{a't, k})(x_{at, k'} - x_{a't, k'}).\]

11.2. Instrument validity

  • Exogeneity
    1. (Cost shifters + policy shock) Plausibly uncorrelated with unobserved amenities
    2. (DIV) Need \( \mathbb{E}[\xi_{at}|(x_{at, k} - x_{a't, k})^2_{a'}, z_{at}] = 0 \).
